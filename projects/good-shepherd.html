An Adjustable Treadmill Grip for Stroke Rehabilitation

Matthew Lindley Adaptive and Assistive Technologies · with Good Shepherd Rehabilitation, Spring 2026

Rehabilitation Engineering Clinical Collaboration 3D Printing
Abstract In Adaptive and Assistive Technologies, our three-person team (Meera Madhavan, Jacob Majerczak and me) designed an adjustable hand grip for stroke survivors doing physical therapy on a treadmill. Clinicians at Good Shepherd Rehabilitation advised us throughout: a neuro outpatient occupational therapist and the physical therapist who leads 3D printing in Good Shepherd's Learns & Creates program. The final concept combines an L-shaped grip with a supportive shield, chosen with a decision matrix.

Finding the Real Problem

Many stroke survivors in PT need upper-extremity support on the treadmill that standard treadmills don't provide. Our first problem statement also tried to address shoulder subluxation. It was too broad, and the early designs came out as bulky, over-complicated rigs. We narrowed the scope to one problem statement:

Design an adjustable hand grip for the treadmill to improve the stability of stroke survivors undergoing physical therapy.

That narrowing was the most useful decision of the project. Every concept after it could be judged against one clear goal.

Concepts

  • Handlebar. A bike-style horizontal grip off the side of the treadmill. It was simple, but it offered one grip, didn't rotate, and did nothing the existing clinic grip didn't already do.
  • Finger holes and gym-style grips. We dropped both. Many of these patients don't have full use of their fingers, and a lat-pulldown-style grip is impractical while walking.
  • L design. An L-shaped rod lets the patient choose a vertical or horizontal grip, with a knob for some rotation.
  • Shield design. A cylindrical grip inside a protective cuff that keeps a weak hand from slipping off and encourages active finger flexion. It clips to the treadmill railing and has a screw adjustment for height.

Designing with Clinicians

Clinician feedback shaped the design at each step. The occupational therapist pushed for a rotating handpiece that allows different angles and grip types, and for height adjustment so one device fits many patients. She suggested two grips as a fallback if full rotation wasn't feasible. She liked the shield, but flagged that getting an impaired hand in and out of it was the real challenge. That turned hand entry into a design requirement.

Final Concept

The selected design merges the L grip and the shield. It offers vertical and horizontal grip options, potential rotation at the mounting pole for adjustable wrist angles, and a shield for patients with weak grip strength. In the design review, we were questioned on two points:

  • our decision matrix used one scale for every criterion, so we rebuilt it with an appropriate measure for each criterion;
  • how much force a patient would actually put through the grip, which raised the question of mounting it further from the rail.

The next steps were the mounting arm (front or side of the treadmill), quantifying patient loads, deciding how much rotation is achievable, 3D modelling, and an FMEA.

What Comes Next

The team set shoulder subluxation aside to keep the scope manageable. I think it's still the bigger unsolved problem, and I'm developing a subluxation sling concept on my own.
